Some of the best-value bottles on the American whiskey shelf say **Bottled-in-Bond** on the label. It sounds like marketing. It isn’t. It’s one of the oldest consumer-protection rules in the United States, and it tells you a lot about what’s in the bottle.

## Where it came from

In the late 1800s, a lot of what was sold as “whiskey” was neutral spirit colored and flavored to look aged. The **Bottled-in-Bond Act of 1897** gave honest producers a way to prove their whiskey was the real thing: follow a strict set of rules under government supervision, and you could say so on the label.

## The rules

To be labeled Bottled-in-Bond, a spirit must be:

1. **Made in one distilling season** (January–June or July–December) by one distiller at one distillery.
2. **Aged at least four years** in a federally bonded warehouse under U.S. government supervision.
3. **Bottled at exactly 100 proof** (50% alcohol).
4. **Labeled with the distillery** where it was made, by its registered plant number, and where it was bottled if that’s somewhere else.

Nothing can be added except water to bring it down to 100 proof.

## What it tells you

- The whiskey is at least four years old, even if there’s no age statement.
- It came from one place and one season, not a blend of sources.
- At 100 proof, there’s enough strength to hold up in an Old Fashioned. Behind the bar, that matters.

## What it doesn’t tell you

Bonded doesn’t mean better. It’s a guarantee of where, when and how strong, not of flavor. Plenty of great whiskey isn’t bonded, and some bonded whiskey is just fine. Taste and decide.

## Try it yourself

Pour a bonded bourbon next to the same brand’s standard bottling, if they make one. Nose both, then add three drops of water to each. Note which one changes more. That’s a lesson in proof you won’t forget.
